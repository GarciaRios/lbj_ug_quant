Lab 5: Sampling and Uncertainty

From one sample to a sampling distribution

Download the student lab template

The policy question

Last week, we treated the Ames housing data as if we could observe the full population. In most policy settings, we cannot.

Suppose the Ames Sustainability Office is planning a residential energy-efficiency program. Home size affects inspection time, retrofit costs, and staffing needs. The office wants to know the average above-ground living area of homes in the city, but collecting information on every home would be expensive.

So the office takes a sample.

That raises a new question:

How much should the city trust an estimate that comes from only one sample?

Today we will use the full Ames dataset as a known population so that we can see exactly what sampling does to our estimates.

Our workflow is:

  1. Take one random sample.
  2. Compare the sample estimate with the population truth.
  3. Repeat the sampling process many times.
  4. Build a sampling distribution.
  5. Measure sampling uncertainty with the standard error.
  6. See how sample size changes precision.

The big idea: a sample statistic is not fixed. If the sample changes, the estimate changes.

Setup

library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

load(url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GarciaRios/govt_3990/gh-pages/Labs/lab4/data/ames.RData"))

ames <- ames |>

    price = SalePrice

The data contain residential home sales in Ames, Iowa, from 2006 through 2010. We will treat these 2,930 sales as our population for today’s simulation.

1. Start with the population

First, calculate the population quantities we are trying to learn from a sample.

population_stats <- ames |>
  summarise(
    N = n(),
    mu_area = mean(area),
    sigma_area = sd(area)
  )

population_stats

Store the population mean and standard deviation so we can use them later.

mu_area <- mean(ames$area)
sigma_area <- sd(ames$area)

And look again at the population distribution:

ggplot(ames, aes(x = area)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 250) +
  labs(
    x = "Living area (square feet)",
    y = "Number of homes",
    title = "Population distribution of living area"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Your turn

  1. What is the population mean living area, \(\mu\)?
  2. What is the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\)?
  3. Describe the shape of the population distribution. Why might that shape matter when we begin taking samples?

Remember: \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are population parameters. In a real policy study, we usually do not know them.

2. Take one sample

Imagine the city can inspect only 50 randomly selected homes.

Choose an integer seed for your group so your work is reproducible. Different groups should choose different seeds.

set.seed(527)  # Replace 527 with an integer chosen by your group.

sample_50 <- ames |>
  slice_sample(n = 50)

Calculate the sample mean and sample standard deviation.

sample_50 |>
  summarise(
    x_bar = mean(area),
    s = sd(area)
  )

Your turn

  1. What is your sample mean, \(\bar{x}\)?
  2. How far is your \(\bar{x}\) from the true population mean \(\mu\)? Report the difference in square feet.
  3. Compare your estimate with another group’s estimate. Are the two sample means identical? Why or why not?

A different random sample usually produces a different value of \(\bar{x}\). That difference is sampling variability.

3. What if we sampled again and again?

One sample gives us one estimate. To understand how much that estimate can vary, imagine repeating the same sampling process.

The code below takes 20 different samples of 50 homes and calculates the mean living area in each sample.

sample_means_20 <- tibble(
  sample_id = 1:20,
  x_bar = replicate(
    20,
    mean(sample(ames$area, size = 50, replace = TRUE))
  )
)

sample_means_20

Plot the 20 estimates. The dashed line is the true population mean.

ggplot(sample_means_20, aes(x = sample_id, y = x_bar)) +
  geom_point(size = 2.5) +
  geom_hline(
    yintercept = mu_area,
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  labs(
    x = "Random sample",
    y = "Sample mean living area",
    title = "Twenty samples produce twenty estimates"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Your turn

  1. What does each point in this graph represent?
  2. Are all 20 estimates on the same side of the true population mean?
  3. What does this graph tell you about treating a single sample mean as if it were exact?

4. Build a sampling distribution

Twenty samples help, but we can do much better with a computer.

Now take 2,000 random samples of size 50 and calculate the mean of each one.

sample_means_50 <- tibble(
  x_bar = replicate(
    2000,
    mean(sample(ames$area, size = 50, replace = TRUE))
  )
)

Visualize the distribution of the 2,000 sample means.

ggplot(sample_means_50, aes(x = x_bar)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 25) +
  geom_vline(
    xintercept = mu_area,
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  labs(
    x = "Sample mean living area",
    y = "Number of samples",
    title = "Sampling distribution of the mean, n = 50"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Summarize the sampling distribution:

sample_means_50 |>
  summarise(
    mean_of_sample_means = mean(x_bar),
    simulated_se = sd(x_bar)
  )

Your turn

  1. What does one observation in sample_means_50 represent?
  2. Describe the shape, center, and spread of the sampling distribution.
  3. Compare the mean of the 2,000 sample means with \(\mu\). What do you notice?

A sampling distribution is a distribution of a statistic, not a distribution of individual homes.

5. Standard deviation versus standard error

The population standard deviation describes variation among individual homes:

\[ \sigma = SD(X). \]

The standard error describes variation among sample means:

\[ SE(\bar{x}) = SD(\bar{x}). \]

Under independent random sampling,

\[ \boxed{SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}} \]

For samples of 50 Ames homes:

theoretical_se_50 <- sigma_area / sqrt(50)
theoretical_se_50

Compare the theoretical standard error with the one generated by our simulation.

sample_means_50 |>
  summarise(
    simulated_se = sd(x_bar),
    theoretical_se = theoretical_se_50
  )

Your turn

  1. How close is the simulated standard error to \(\sigma/\sqrt{50}\)?
  2. In one sentence, explain the difference between standard deviation and standard error.
  3. Why is the standard error much smaller than the population standard deviation?

6. What changes when the sample gets larger?

Create sampling distributions using samples of size 10, 50, and 100.

sample_means_10 <- tibble(
  n = "n = 10",
  x_bar = replicate(
    2000,
    mean(sample(ames$area, size = 10, replace = TRUE))
  )
)

sample_means_100 <- tibble(
  n = "n = 100",
  x_bar = replicate(
    2000,
    mean(sample(ames$area, size = 100, replace = TRUE))
  )
)

sampling_sizes <- bind_rows(
  sample_means_10,
  sample_means_50 |> mutate(n = "n = 50"),
  sample_means_100
) |>
  mutate(
    n = factor(n, levels = c("n = 10", "n = 50", "n = 100"))
  )

Plot all three sampling distributions using the same horizontal scale.

ggplot(sampling_sizes, aes(x = x_bar)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 25) +
  facet_wrap(~ n, ncol = 1) +
  geom_vline(
    xintercept = mu_area,
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  labs(
    x = "Sample mean living area",
    y = "Number of samples",
    title = "Larger samples produce more precise estimates"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Compare their centers and simulated standard errors.

sampling_sizes |>
  group_by(n) |>
  summarise(
    center = mean(x_bar),
    simulated_se = sd(x_bar)
  )

And compare with the theoretical standard errors:

tibble(
  n = c(10, 50, 100),
  theoretical_se = sigma_area / sqrt(n)
)

Your turn

  1. What happens to the center of the sampling distribution as \(n\) increases?
  2. What happens to the spread as \(n\) increases?
  3. What happens to the shape as \(n\) increases, especially given that the population itself is somewhat right-skewed?
  4. Which sample size gives the most precise estimate of the population mean? How can you tell from both the graph and the standard error?
  5. Suppose the city wants to cut the standard error in half. By what factor would it need to increase the sample size? Explain using the \(\sqrt{n}\) in the denominator.

Larger samples do not guarantee the exact population value. They make large sampling errors less common.

7. Turn sampling uncertainty into a policy statement

Suppose the Sustainability Office uses a random sample of 50 homes to estimate average living area.

How likely is a sample mean to overstate the true population mean by at least 100 square feet?

Under the Central Limit Theorem,

\[ \bar{X} \approx N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right). \]

Use the normal model for the sampling distribution:

prob_over_by_100 <- 1 - pnorm(
  mu_area + 100,
  mean = mu_area,
  sd = theoretical_se_50
)

prob_over_by_100

Now compare that model-based answer with our simulated sampling distribution:

sample_means_50 |>
  summarise(
    simulated_probability = mean(x_bar >= mu_area + 100)
  )

Your turn

  1. Interpret prob_over_by_100 in words for the Sustainability Office.
  2. How close is the simulated probability to the probability predicted by the CLT?
  3. If overestimating average home size leads the city to over-budget staff time or retrofit resources, what does this probability tell the program manager?
  4. What could the city do to reduce this sampling uncertainty?

On Your Own: Estimate average home price

The Sustainability Office is now considering a housing program whose budget depends on home prices rather than home size.

This time, you will conduct the analysis.

A. One sample

  1. Take a random sample of 25 homes and calculate the sample mean sale price. Treat this as your point estimate of the population mean.
  1. Calculate the actual population mean sale price so you can evaluate your estimate. How far away was your sample estimate?

B. Build a sampling distribution

  1. Simulate 2,000 sample means using samples of size 25. Store them in a tibble called price_means_25.
  1. Plot the sampling distribution. Describe its shape and center.
  1. Calculate the simulated standard error and compare it with

\[ \frac{\sigma_{price}}{\sqrt{25}}. \]

C. Increase the sample size

  1. Repeat the simulation using samples of 100 homes and store the results as price_means_100.
  1. Compare the sampling distributions for \(n=25\) and \(n=100\). Which gives the city a more precise estimate, and why?

D. Policy recommendation

  1. Write 2–3 sentences to the program manager explaining why a larger sample can improve a policy estimate even though no single sample is guaranteed to equal the population truth.

Do not stop at “the standard error is smaller.” Explain what a smaller standard error means for the reliability of the policy estimate.
