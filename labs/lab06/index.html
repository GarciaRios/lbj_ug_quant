Lab 6: Confidence Intervals

From a sample estimate to a policy range

Download the student lab template

The policy question

The Ames Sustainability Office is preparing a residential energy-efficiency program. Home size affects inspection time, retrofit materials, and staffing needs.

The office cannot inspect every home, so it uses a random sample to estimate the average living area of homes in Ames.

A point estimate is useful, but a program manager needs more:

How precise is the estimate, and is it precise enough to make a policy decision?

Today we will move from a single sample mean to a confidence interval and then ask what that interval means for planning.

A confidence interval is not just a calculation. It is a way to communicate estimate + uncertainty.

Setup

library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

load(url("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GarciaRios/govt_3990/gh-pages/Labs/lab4/data/ames.RData"))

ames <- ames |>
  rename(
    area = Gr.Liv.Area,
    price = SalePrice
  )

We will continue treating the 2,930 Ames home sales as a known population so we can check how well our confidence-interval procedure works.

Store the population mean for later:

mu_area <- mean(ames$area)
mu_area

1. Start with one policy sample

Suppose the city can inspect 60 randomly selected homes.

Choose an integer seed for your group. Different groups should use different seeds.

set.seed(614)  # Replace 614 with a seed chosen by your group.

n <- 60

sample_60 <- ames |>
  slice_sample(n = n)

Summarize the sample:

sample_stats <- sample_60 |>
  summarise(
    x_bar = mean(area),
    s = sd(area)
  )

sample_stats

Your turn

  1. What is your point estimate of the population mean living area?

  2. What does the sample standard deviation tell you about the homes in your sample?

  3. Compare your sample mean with another group. Why should the city expect different analysts to get slightly different estimates even when both use good random samples?

2. From standard error to margin of error

For a mean, our estimated standard error is

\[ SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

Calculate it:

x_bar <- mean(sample_60$area)
s <- sd(sample_60$area)

se <- s / sqrt(n)
se

Because the population standard deviation is unknown, we use a t critical value.

For a 95% confidence interval with \(n=60\):

t_star <- qt(
  0.975,
  df = n - 1
)

t_star

The margin of error is

\[ ME = t^* \times SE. \]

me <- t_star * se
me

Your turn

  1. What is the standard error of your sample mean? Interpret it in context.

  2. What is the 95% critical value \(t^*\) for your sample?

  3. What is your margin of error? What units is it measured in?

Keep these three quantities distinct:

  • SD: variation among individual homes
  • SE: expected variation among sample means
  • ME: how far we extend from the point estimate to form this interval

3. Construct the 95% confidence interval

A 95% confidence interval for a population mean is

\[ \boxed{ \bar{x} \pm t^*\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} } \]

Calculate the lower and upper bounds:

lower_95 <- x_bar - me
upper_95 <- x_bar + me

c(
  lower = lower_95,
  estimate = x_bar,
  upper = upper_95
)

Your turn

  1. Report your 95% confidence interval.

  2. Write a one-sentence interpretation for the Ames Sustainability Office. Your sentence should refer to the population mean living area, not individual homes.

  3. Does your interval contain the actual Ames population mean, \(\mu\)? Remember that in a real application, the city would not know the true value.

4. What does “95% confidence” actually mean?

Before running any more code, choose the best interpretation:

Quick vote

A 95% confidence interval means:

A. There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is inside the interval we just calculated.

B. 95% of individual Ames homes fall inside the interval.

C. If we repeatedly took random samples and built intervals the same way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true population mean.

D. The sample mean has a 95% chance of being correct.

Your turn

  1. Which answer did you choose? Explain why the other interpretations are wrong.

The key is repeated sampling. The parameter \(\mu\) is fixed. The interval changes from sample to sample.

5. Let the class generate different intervals

Because each group chose a different seed, each group should have a slightly different interval.

Class activity

Write your group’s three values where everyone can see them:

\[ \text{lower bound} \qquad \bar{x} \qquad \text{upper bound} \]

Then compare across groups.

Your turn

  1. Are the intervals identical across groups?

  2. Do they all have exactly the same width? Why might widths differ slightly even though every group used \(n=60\)?

  3. Did every group capture the true population mean? If one did not, does that mean the group made a mistake?

6. Simulate 100 confidence intervals

Now we will automate the class activity.

This helper function draws one sample of 60 homes and returns a 95% confidence interval.

draw_ci <- function() {
  x <- sample(
    ames$area,
    size = n,
    replace = FALSE
  )

  x_bar_i <- mean(x)
  s_i <- sd(x)
  se_i <- s_i / sqrt(n)
  t_star_i <- qt(0.975, df = n - 1)

  c(
    x_bar = x_bar_i,
    lower = x_bar_i - t_star_i * se_i,
    upper = x_bar_i + t_star_i * se_i
  )
}

Generate 100 intervals:

ci_100 <- replicate(
  100,
  draw_ci()
) |>
  t() |>
  as.data.frame() |>
  tibble::as_tibble() |>
  mutate(
    interval_id = row_number(),
    captures_mu = lower <= mu_area & upper >= mu_area
  )

Look at the first few:

ci_100 |>
  slice(1:8)

Calculate the proportion that capture the true mean:

ci_100 |>
  summarise(
    capture_rate = mean(captures_mu)
  )

Your turn

  1. What proportion of your 100 intervals captured \(\mu\)?

  2. Is it exactly 95%? Should it have to be exactly 95% in a set of only 100 intervals?

  3. If we simulated 100,000 intervals instead, what capture rate would you expect to see approximately?

7. See confidence in action

Plot the first 30 intervals.

ci_100 |>
  filter(interval_id <= 30) |>
  ggplot(
    aes(
      y = interval_id,
      x = lower,
      xend = upper,
      yend = interval_id,
      color = captures_mu
    )
  ) +
  geom_segment(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(
    aes(x = x_bar),
    size = 2
  ) +
  geom_vline(
    xintercept = mu_area,
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  labs(
    x = "Living area (square feet)",
    y = "Confidence interval",
    color = "Captures true mean?",
    title = "Thirty 95% confidence intervals"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

Your turn

  1. What distinguishes the intervals that fail to capture \(\mu\)?

  2. Does a failed interval look obviously “bad” before we reveal the population mean?

  3. Why is this graph a better explanation of 95% confidence than saying “there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this interval”?

8. Confidence versus precision

Using the same sample, construct 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence intervals.

ci_levels <- tibble(
  confidence = c(0.90, 0.95, 0.99),
  t_star = c(
    qt(0.95, df = n - 1),
    qt(0.975, df = n - 1),
    qt(0.995, df = n - 1)
  )
) |>
  mutate(
    margin_of_error = t_star * se,
    lower = x_bar - margin_of_error,
    upper = x_bar + margin_of_error,
    width = upper - lower
  )

ci_levels

Predict before looking at the output

Which interval should be widest: 90%, 95%, or 99%?

Your turn

  1. What happens to interval width as confidence increases?

  2. Why does greater confidence require a wider interval when the data and sample size stay fixed?

  3. A program manager says, “I want 99% confidence and a narrower interval, but I do not want to collect more data.” Explain the tradeoff.

9. Is the estimate precise enough for policy?

Suppose the Sustainability Office says:

We can plan confidently only if the margin of error for average living area is no more than 75 square feet.

Compare that policy requirement with your 95% interval.

Your turn

  1. Does your current sample of 60 homes meet the city’s precision requirement?

  2. If it does not, what can the city change to make the interval narrower without lowering its confidence level?

  3. Why would “just report the sample mean and ignore the interval” be a poor response to the city’s concern?

10. Plan a larger sample

For planning purposes, suppose the city uses the sample standard deviation \(s\) as its best guess of population variability.

A rough sample-size calculation for a 95% margin of error is

\[ n \approx \left( \frac{1.96s}{ME} \right)^2. \]

Calculate the sample size needed for a target margin of error of 75 square feet:

target_me <- 75

n_needed <- ceiling(
  (1.96 * s / target_me)^2
)

n_needed

Your turn

  1. How many homes should the city plan to sample to target a margin of error of about 75 square feet?

  2. Why do we round the required sample size up rather than down?

  3. If the city wanted to cut the margin of error roughly in half, about how much larger would the sample need to be? Connect your answer to the square-root relationship from last week.

On Your Own: Estimate mean home price

Now suppose a housing assistance program needs an estimate of the average sale price of Ames homes.

Use a new random sample of 80 homes.

A. Build a 95% confidence interval

  1. Draw a random sample of 80 homes and calculate \(\bar{x}\), \(s\), \(SE\), \(t^*\), margin of error, and the 95% confidence interval for mean sale price.
  1. Write the interval in dollars and interpret it in one sentence for the housing program manager.

B. Evaluate the interval

  1. Calculate the true population mean sale price and determine whether your interval captured it.
  1. Construct 90% and 99% intervals using the same sample. Compare their widths with your 95% interval.

C. Policy recommendation

The program manager wants a margin of error no larger than $15,000 at 95% confidence.

  1. Does your current sample of 80 homes meet that precision target?

  2. Using your sample standard deviation as the planning value, estimate how many homes the city would need to sample to target a $15,000 margin of error.

  1. Write 2–3 sentences to the program manager explaining whether the current sample is precise enough and, if not, what you recommend.

A useful policy conclusion reports both the estimate and how much uncertainty remains around it.
