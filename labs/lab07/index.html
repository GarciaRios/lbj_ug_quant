Lab 7: Inference for Numerical Data

Hypothesis testing and confidence intervals

Download the student lab template

North Carolina births

In 2004, the state of North Carolina released a large data set containing information on births recorded in the state. These data are useful for researchers and policymakers interested in maternal and infant health.

We will work with a random sample of observations from this data set.

library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

load(url(
  "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GarciaRios/govt_3990/gh-pages/Labs/lab5/nc.RData"

The data include the following variables:

Variable Description
fage Father’s age in years
mage Mother’s age in years
mature Maternal age category

visits Number of prenatal visits
marital Mother’s marital status at birth

glimpse(nc)

Exploratory analysis

Consider the possible relationship between a mother’s smoking status and the birth weight of her baby.

A graph is a useful first step.

ggplot(nc, aes(x = habit, y = weight)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  labs(
    x = "Mother's smoking status",
    y = "Birth weight (pounds)",
    title = "Birth weight by maternal smoking status"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

We can also compare the group means:

nc |>
  group_by(habit) |>
  summarise(
    mean_weight = mean(weight, na.rm = TRUE),
    sd_weight = sd(weight, na.rm = TRUE),
    n = sum(!is.na(weight))
  )

  1. What are the cases in this data set? Approximately how many cases are in the sample?

  2. What does the boxplot suggest about the relationship between smoking status and birth weight?

  3. How do the average birth weights of the two groups compare?

There is an observed difference. The next question is whether that difference is large enough to be difficult to explain by sampling variability alone.

Inference

Before conducting a hypothesis test, consider whether the conditions for inference are reasonably satisfied.

nc |>

Now write the hypotheses for testing whether average birth weight differs between babies born to smoking and nonsmoking mothers.

  1. Write the null and alternative hypotheses.

The null hypothesis should represent no difference between the two population means.

Conduct the hypothesis test

We can conduct the test directly with t.test():

birthweight_test <- t.test(
  weight ~ habit,
  data = nc,
  alternative = "two.sided",
  conf.level = 0.95
)

birthweight_test

  1. Record the two sample means, the test statistic, and the p-value.

  2. Using a significance level of (= 0.05), do you reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis?

  3. Interpret the conclusion in the context of birth weight and smoking status.

A small p-value provides evidence against the null hypothesis. It does not tell us the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Confidence interval

The output above also reports a 95% confidence interval for the difference between the two population means.

  1. Record the confidence interval.

  2. Does the interval contain 0? Does this agree with your conclusion from the hypothesis test?

  3. Interpret the interval in context.

A two-sided test at (= 0.05) and a 95% confidence interval should lead to the same inferential conclusion.

From statistical evidence to policy interpretation

Suppose a public-health agency is deciding whether smoking during pregnancy deserves attention in maternal-health programming.

The analysis above can tell us whether birth weight differs across the two groups in this sample and whether the difference is statistically distinguishable from zero.

It cannot, by itself, prove that smoking caused the difference. These are observational data, and the two groups may differ in other ways that are related to birth weight.

  1. Name one or two possible confounding variables that could matter here.

  2. In 2–3 sentences, summarize what this analysis contributes to a policy discussion and what it does not establish.

Group analysis

Now apply the same workflow to a new question.

Each group will analyze one relationship and prepare a two-minute report for the class.

Group A: Smoking and pregnancy length

Do mothers who report smoking and nonsmoking mothers have different average pregnancy lengths?

  • response: weeks
  • group: habit

Group B: Maternal age and prenatal care

Does the average number of prenatal visits differ between younger and mature mothers?

  • response: visits
  • group: mature

Group C: Racial disparities in prenatal care

Does the average number of prenatal visits differ between mothers coded as White and mothers coded as not White?

  • response: visits
  • group: whitemom

This analysis identifies a disparity. It does not establish that race itself causes the difference.

Group D: Marital status and birth weight

Does average birth weight differ according to the mother’s marital status at birth?

  • response: weight
  • group: marital

Group E: Maternal age and pregnancy weight gain

Does average pregnancy weight gain differ between younger and mature mothers?

  • response: gained
  • group: mature

What each group should do

  1. Create one useful visualization.
  2. Calculate the mean, standard deviation, and sample size for each group.
  3. State (H_0) and (H_A).
  4. Conduct the two-sample t-test.
  5. Report the p-value and 95% confidence interval.
  6. Write a brief conclusion in context.
  7. Identify one limitation or caution.
